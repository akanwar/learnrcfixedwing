Gliders, Flying Wings, Biplanes & Other Shapes

Nothing in chapters 1–7 was trainer-specific — the same physics runs every shape. What changes per configuration is which term dominates. This volume walks the family tree with one worked number for each branch.

Gliders: the mission is efficiency itself

Delete the motor and the design problem becomes pure aerodynamics: minimum sink for thermals, best glide for distance. The Math Shelf’s drag polar gives the ceiling directly:

L/Dmax = ½√(π·e·AR / CD0)
trainer: ½√(π × 0.8 × 6.5 / 0.035) ≈ 11  ·  glider (AR 10, e 0.85, clean CD0 0.02) ≈ 18

Every design choice follows from that formula: AR up (with Deep Dive 3’s spar bill — root bending grows with span), parasite drag down (clean fuselage, folding prop), wing loading down to 20–30 g/dm² so the circles stay slow and small. And one non-obvious trick: ballast. Adding weight does not change L/D — the polar is loading-independent — it just shifts the whole speed range faster. Competition pilots bolt lead in on windy days to penetrate, and drop it for weak-lift evenings.

Flying wings: stability without a tail

Chapter 3 said the tail keeps the CG–NP relationship honest. Remove the tail and you must buy pitch stability another way: sweep + washout (the twisted-down tips act as a tail a short lever behind the CG) or a reflexed airfoil on a straight “plank” (the upturned trailing edge does the same job in-section). Both cost a little lift and efficiency — there is no free tail.

The bookkeeping change is that “25% of the chord” stops being one place: on a swept, tapered wing you must first find the mean aerodynamic chord and where it sits. For a swept wing with root chord cr, tip chord ct, taper λ = ct/cr:

MAC = ⅔ cr (1+λ+λ²)/(1+λ)  ·  yMAC = (b/6)(1+2λ)/(1+λ)  ·  xLE,MAC = yMAC tanΛLE
Worked: λ = 0.6 → MAC = 245 mm at y = 275 mm; LE of MAC sits 275·tan 24° = 122 mm aft of the root LE.
CG at 15–20% MAC → 159–171 mm aft of the root leading edge.
Two honest cautions. Washout on a swept wing is structural stability equipment, not a finishing touch — 3–4° is typical, and a wing built without it will tuck. And the allowable CG range is narrower than a tailed model’s (aim 15–20% MAC, sweep-and-twist dependent — the elevons’ short lever arm forgives little): sneak aft in 5 mm steps over many flights, never in one. Elevons mix aileron + elevator in the transmitter; start with low rates on both.
Your turnYour friend’s plank (straight wing, reflexed section) balances fine at 15% MAC. Your swept wing won’t settle at the “same” 15%. What did the sweep change?
On the plank, MAC sits at the wing — xLE,MAC = 0, so “15%” measured from the root LE is correct as-is. Sweep moves the MAC 122 mm aft of the root LE in our example: measuring 15% from the root leading edge would put the CG ≈120 mm too far forward — a wing so nose-heavy the elevons run out of authority in the flare. Same rule, different datum: always construct the MAC first.

V-tails: two surfaces do two jobs

A V-tail replaces three surfaces with two, saving a little drag and weight at the cost of mixed controls (“ruddervators”) and coupled behaviour. The classic sizing rule: give the V the same total area as the horizontal + vertical tails it replaces, angled so the projections match:

SV-tail = Sh + Sv = 640 + 300 = 940 cm²  ·  dihedral θ = atan√(Sv/Sh) = atan√(300/640) ≈ 34° from horizontal

The trainer’s tail volumes (chapter 3) survive unchanged — you are re-packaging the same stabilising power. Transmitters mix ruddervators natively; expect a touch less yaw bite and slightly odd knife-edge behaviour, which is why trainers keep the conventional cross.

Biplanes: span in half the space

Two wings fit a hangar-limited span. The physics tax is interference: each wing flies in the other’s downwash, so two wings deliver roughly 0.85–0.9 of their isolated lift capability (Munk’s classic result — improving as the gap grows; keep it at least one chord). Practical recipe: size total area with chapter 2’s wing-loading table plus ≈10–15% to cover interference, keep gap ≥ 1 chord, add a little stagger (upper wing forward) for pilot view and gentler stall, and budget grams for the cabane and interplane struts — the biplane’s real cost is rigging weight and drag, which is why it suits aerobats (roll inertia!) more than trainers.

Canards: the tail that flies first

Put the small surface ahead of the wing and it lifts instead of pushing down — seductive on paper. The catch is certification-grade: for a canard to be safe, the front surface must always stall before the main wing (higher loading and incidence up front), which means the main wing can never reach its own CLmax — you pay in landing speed for the tidy stall. CG range is tight, and the “tail volume” bookkeeping runs with reversed signs. File under: second airplane, not first.

Your turnSame 1.5 kg mass, same 45 g/dm² loading, but as a biplane with 0.85 interference efficiency. How much total wing area do you build?
The aircraft still needs the lift of 33.2 dm² of effective wing; at 0.85 effectiveness you build 33.2 / 0.85 ≈ 39 dm² of actual area — but each of the two wings spans far less than 1.47 m: at AR 5 per wing, each panel spans just √(5 × 0.195) ≈ 0.99 m. A metre-wide box that flies like a metre-and-a-half monoplane — that is the biplane trade in one line.
