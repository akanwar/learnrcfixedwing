Chapter 05

Understand Airfoils

The wing section shapes the pressure field, the lift range, the stall manners, and even how you build. Choose it like an engineer, not a fan.

How designers actually choose

Nobody asks “which airfoil is best?” They ask: how heavy is the plane, how fast will it cruise, how slowly must it land, will it fly inverted, how easy must it be to build, and how small is the chord (which sets Reynolds number)? Answer those, and the airfoil almost picks itself. Choose the section after mission, mass, and wing loading are known — never before.

One big idea: the wing pushes air down, so the air pushes the wing up (Newton’s third law). The airfoil decides how efficiently that trade happens, over what speed range, and how gently it fails at the stall.

Reading a section

Chord line: straight line, leading edge to trailing edge. Camber: the curvature of the mean line between upper and lower surfaces — a symmetric section has curved surfaces but zero camber. Thickness: the max gap between surfaces; thick = room for a strong spar. Camber buys lift at low angles; its bill is a nose-down pitching moment the tail must carry.

Your turnYour trainer cruises at Re ≈ 1.8×10⁵. Rank Clark Y, S1223, and NACA 0012 for it — and what changes if you halve the chord?
For a trainer at that Re: Clark Y first (docile, buildable), 0012 pointless (no camber = no free lift), S1223 overkill (trim drag, build pain). Halving the chord halves Re to ≈90k — trouble-zone territory where the S1223/E387 class of low-Re sections earns its keep.

The polite fiction of the lift curve

All these sections obey the same story: lift rises nearly linearly with angle of attack (≈0.08–0.1 per degree), peaks at CLmax, then separates. Camber slides the line left (lift at zero angle); thickness and Reynolds number shape how sharp the peak is. The full curve, with the stall annotated, lives on the math shelf.
