Chapter 05

Understand Airfoils

The wing section shapes the pressure field, the lift range, the stall manners, and even how you build. Choose it like an engineer, not a fan.

How designers actually choose

Nobody asks “which airfoil is best?” They ask: how heavy is the plane, how fast will it cruise, how slowly must it land, will it fly inverted, how easy must it be to build, and how small is the chord (which sets Reynolds number)? Answer those, and the airfoil almost picks itself. Choose the section after mission, mass, and wing loading are known — never before.

One big idea: the wing pushes air down, so the air pushes the wing up (Newton’s third law). The airfoil decides how efficiently that trade happens, over what speed range, and how gently it fails at the stall.

Reading a section

Chord line: straight line, leading edge to trailing edge. Camber: the curvature of the mean line between upper and lower surfaces — a symmetric section has curved surfaces but zero camber. Thickness: the max gap between surfaces; thick = room for a strong spar. Camber buys lift at low angles; its bill is a nose-down pitching moment the tail must carry.

Your turnYour trainer cruises at Re ≈ 1.8×10⁵. Rank Clark Y, S1223, and NACA 0012 for it — and what changes if you halve the chord?
For a trainer at that Re: Clark Y first (docile, buildable), 0012 pointless (no camber = no free lift), S1223 overkill (trim drag, build pain). Halving the chord halves Re to ≈90k — trouble-zone territory where the S1223/E387 class of low-Re sections earns its keep.

The families, in profile

True-scale outlines from the published coordinates. The dashed orange line is the mean camber line — the single most personality-defining curve on each plot. Green marks max thickness (spar room), orange marks max camber.

Clark Y

Clark Y — the friendly builder’s airfoil. The nearly straight aft lower surface sits flat on the building board, which is half the reason generations learned on it. Moderate camber gives useful lift at small angles; the fat round nose keeps flow attached as angle rises. Watch out: not great inverted, and it gets draggy when pushed fast.

NACA 2412

NACA 2412 — the balanced all-rounder. The name is the geometry: 2% camber at 40% chord, 12% thick. A deliberate compromise — reasonably efficient, reasonably gentle, sporty enough. The curved bottom needs rib tabs or a jig, and upright vs inverted trim will differ.

Selig S1223

S1223 — high lift at low speed, and it looks it. That deep camber bends the airflow aggressively for huge lift at model Reynolds numbers — at the cost of a strong nose-down pitching moment (the tail pays for it), fiddly undercambered construction, and real drag at speed. For slow flyers, STOL, and payload lifting.

NACA 0012

NACA 0012 — the same shape top and bottom. Zero camber means zero lift at zero angle: it only works when you give it an angle, and it works identically inverted. That symmetry is why it flies aerobatics and why it is the default for nearly every tail surface ever built.

Eppler E387

E387 — the low-Reynolds thoroughbred. Thin (9.1%) with gentle camber, designed specifically for the Reynolds numbers where model gliders live. Efficient sections punish sloppy building — accurate ribs and a smooth skin matter more here. And remember: “Eppler” is a family, not a shape. E423 is a high-lift barn door, E472 is symmetric aerobatic. Always cite the number.

The polite fiction of the lift curve

All these sections obey the same story: lift rises nearly linearly with angle of attack (≈0.08–0.1 per degree), peaks at CLmax, then separates. Camber slides the line left (lift at zero angle); thickness and Reynolds number shape how sharp the peak is. The full curve, with the stall annotated, lives on the math shelf.
