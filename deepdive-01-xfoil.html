Aerodynamic Analysis & the XFOIL Workflow

Chapter 5 chose an airfoil from tables. This volume shows you how to interrogate one yourself — polars for your section at your Reynolds number, in minutes, free — and how to know when the answer is lying to you.

Why the polar must be yours

Published airfoil data is usually measured or computed near Re 10⁶. Your trainer never gets close:

Re ≈ 68 000 × V × c  →  cruise 12 m/s: ≈185k · approach 10.5: ≈162k · stall 8.1: ≈125k

Below ≈2×10⁵ the boundary layer is part laminar, separation bubbles appear, and CLmax and drag both move — sometimes a lot (the Math Shelf covers why). So the question is never “how good is this airfoil?” but “how good is it at 125k–185k?” Three ways to answer, in rising order of effort:

SourceWhat it isTrust level
UIUC low-speed testsWind-tunnel measurements at exactly our Re range (m-selig.ae.illinois.edu)The reference. If UIUC measured your section, believe UIUC
XFOILDrela’s panel code + boundary-layer model; runs any section at any Re in secondsVery good below stall; optimistic near CLmax at low Re
airfoiltools.comPre-computed XFOIL polars online, Re 50k–1MSame physics as XFOIL — convenient, but you can’t change Ncrit or step size

An XFOIL session, keystroke by keystroke

XFOIL is a free command-line program (web.mit.edu/drela; builds exist for Windows, macOS and Linux). Download your section’s coordinates from UIUC (e.g. s1223.dat), put it next to the program, and type exactly this:

You typeWhat happens
LOAD s1223.datreads the coordinate file
PANErepanels the surface smoothly — always do this for UIUC files
OPERenters the analysis menu
VISC 185000viscous mode at Re = 185 000 (your cruise — change it per case)
ITER 100more solver iterations; low-Re cases converge slowly
PACCstarts polar accumulation — give it a save filename when asked
ASEQ -4 14 0.5sweeps angle of attack −4° to 14° in half-degree steps
PACCcloses the polar file; open it in any plotting tool or spreadsheet
If it won’t converge near stall (the numbers stop settling): that itself is information — the boundary layer is letting go. Take the last converged point as “about here”, not the exact stall. Repeat the whole run at 125k and 162k; three polars bracket your flight envelope.

Low-Re honesty: where XFOIL flatters you

Three systematic lies to expect at model Reynolds numbers. 1) CLmax comes out optimistic — often 10–15% above wind-tunnel truth at 100–200k, so derate it before believing a stall speed. 2) Laminar separation bubbles: XFOIL models them, but their drag penalty is sensitive to Ncrit — the transition parameter. The default 9 represents a calm tunnel; a hand-launched model in real air behaves closer to 7–9, a rough/turbulated wing closer to 4–5. Run both and treat the spread as your uncertainty. 3) Anything past the stall is fiction — panel methods cannot do separated flow.

The professional habit: compute in XFOIL, then anchor against the nearest UIUC measurement. Example anchor: the S1223 was measured at CLmax ≈ 2.2 at Re 200k (Selig & Guglielmo’s original paper) — if your XFOIL run says 2.4–2.5, you are seeing lie #1, now calibrated.

Your turnYour XFOIL run of the trainer’s Clark Y at Re 185k reports CLmax = 1.32. What stall speed should you actually plan for?
Derate the 2-D number ≈10–15% for XFOIL optimism and a little more for 3-D wing effects — landing near the course’s CLmax 1.1 is honest. Vs = √(2W/ρSCLmax): with 1.1 you get the familiar 8.1 m/s; believing 1.32 would have promised 7.4 m/s and delivered a surprise flare. Plan with the derated number; enjoy any bonus.

The analysis ladder — XFOIL, XFLR5, CFD

This section is the sixth planned volume folded into its natural home: when is each level of analysis worth the effort?

RungToolModelsCostWorth it when…
1Hand rules (ch 2–4)lift equation, tail volumes, static marginminutesalways — every design starts and sanity-ends here

4RANS CFD (OpenFOAM…)full Navier–Stokes, separated flowweeks + meshing skillalmost never for a conventional model. Blended wings, ducted installations, prop–airframe interaction — maybe
The honest hierarchy: a trainer designed on rungs 1–2 and verified with a wattmeter and a maiden flight beats a trainer “optimised” on rung 4 by someone who never measured anything. Escalate only when a specific question resists the rung you are on — XFLR5’s VLM knows nothing about stall, and CFD answers arrive slower than a build-and-fly experiment at model scale.
Your turnXFLR5 places your trainer’s neutral point at 44% of chord; chapter 3’s screening formula said 46%. Which do you trust, and does the difference matter?
They agree to 2% c̄ — that is agreement for screening tools (the fuselage, ignored by both to different degrees, moves the NP more than this). With the CG at 25%, static margin is 19–21% either way: same airplane, same conservative maiden. Trust the more conservative number and move on to building.
