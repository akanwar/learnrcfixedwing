3D-Printed Fixed Wings

A printer turns airfoil coordinates into ribs-and-skin structure overnight — perfect sections, repeatable crashes-and-reprints. The catch is mass, and the fix is a material trick plus a division of labour with a carbon spar. Numbers first, as always.

The material trick: LW-PLA

Ordinary PLA is dense (1.24 g/cm³) — a solid-skinned PLA wing sinks the mass budget instantly. LW-PLA (“lightweight”, foaming PLA) contains an agent that expands the extruded bead 2–3× at around 230–260 °C. You compensate by printing at reduced flow (≈40–60%), and the cured wall lands at an effective density around 0.5–0.65 g/cm³ — half of regular PLA. Every brand foams differently with temperature, so the first print is always a calibration: a single-wall cube at each temperature step, measured and weighed, before any wing part.

Honest mass math for the trainer’s wing

skin area ≈ 2.05 × S = 2.05 × 33.2 dm² ≈ 0.68 m²
single 0.5 mm wall at ≈0.6 g/cm³ → 0.68 × 0.0005 × 600 ≈ 204 g of skin
+ ≈25% for internal ribs, joins and the spar pocket → ≈255 g printed wing

A built-up balsa wing of this size finishes around 200–240 g covered — so the printed wing is competitive but a shade heavier. All-up mass drifts to ≈1550 g and wing loading from 45 to ≈46 g/dm²: still squarely in the trainer band (chapter 2’s table absorbs it). The honest conclusion cuts both ways: printing suits trainers and sport models fine, and suits floaters badly — you cannot print your way to 25 g/dm².

Your turnRun the same skin math for the park-flyer preset (600 g, S ≈ 18.6 dm²). Does a printed wing still make sense?
Skin area ≈ 2.05 × 0.186 = 0.38 m² → at the trainer recipe (0.5 mm, 0.6 g/cm³) that is ≈114 g of skin — call it ≈140 g with ribs — where a built-up park-flyer wing finishes nearer 50–60 g. Even at the aggressive limit (0.4 mm wall, maximum foam ≈0.5 g/cm³) the skin is ≈76 g. The wall has a minimum thickness whether the model is big or small, so the smaller the aircraft, the worse the printed skin’s fixed cost: loading jumps 32 → ≈37 g/dm² and the “park flyer” becomes a small sport model. Print the trainer; build the floater.

Division of labour: skin for shape, spar for bending

Printed parts have a grain: the bond between layers is the weak direction — plan on 50–70% of in-plane strength, worse with heavy foaming. Wing sections print standing up (span along the printer’s Z), which puts the layer joints exactly where root bending wants to tear the wing apart. So printed-wing design splits the job the way Deep Dive 3’s I-beam logic says to:

JobCarried byWhy it works
Bending (the 13.8 N·m from DD3)Carbon tube spar in a printed pocket — the same 10×8 mm tube DD3 sized at SF 2.5continuous fibre across every section joint; the print never sees the cap loads
Torsion + shapethe closed printed skina closed cell is a printed D-box all the way around; layer bonds carry shear, which they tolerate far better than tension
Local dents, handlingwall thickness + internal ribs the slicer buildsperfect airfoil accuracy is the printer’s one free lunch — chapter 5’s low-Re sections love an accurate leading edge

Orientation, sections and joints

Practical rules: split the span into sections that fit your printer’s height; put joints away from the root where the bending moment peaks (DD3’s M curve falls toward the tips); align sections with the spar tube itself plus a small printed pin or tab; glue with medium CA or 30-minute epoxy on clean, lightly-sanded faces. Put the slicer’s seam on the trailing edge, and orient any cosmetic sanding along the airflow — through-sanding a 0.5 mm foamed wall is easy, so resist the urge to polish.

Two failure modes to expect and shrug off: a section that delaminates in a hard landing (reprint it — that is the whole point: 40 g of filament and tonight’s print queue), and UV + heat softening — LW-PLA sags in a hot car faster than a covered balsa wing ever will. Store wings out of parked-car heat, and treat a summer-noon tarmac like the enemy it is.
Your turnYour calibration cube weighs 42% of what solid-flow PLA would. Your slicer is set to 55% flow. What happens to the wing — and which direction do you adjust?
The filament is foaming more than your flow setting assumes (it only “needs” ≈42%), so at 55% you are extruding ≈30% too much material: a heavier wing (the 204 g skin becomes ≈265 g) with bulging over-extruded walls. Drop flow toward ≈42–45% and re-print the cube to confirm. The cube is cheap; the wing is not.
