Chapter 07

Systems, Test & Fly

A maiden flight is a controlled experiment: the goal is not to prove the airplane perfect, but to collect safe evidence — and bring it home.

The power system is one circuit

Battery → ESC → motor is the high-current loop: keep it short, thick, and compact. The ESC (electronic speed control) is the throttle’s power valve; its BEC (battery-eliminator circuit) sends 5 V up the throttle lead — that one plug is the receiver’s only power source. Everything past the receiver is the low-current signal side: thin, light, routed away from power wires, antennas at 90°.

Prop sizes like 10×6 are the hobby’s one stubbornly imperial number: inches, diameter × pitch — a 10×6 is 10″ across and, like a screw thread, would advance 6″ per revolution.

US charts speak W/lb: multiply by 2.2 to get W/kg — the classic “50 W/lb trainer” rule of thumb is ≈110 W/kg, near this table’s low end.

Kv is not a power rating, C-rating is a claim not a promise, and prop charts beat guessing. Choose motor + prop + ESC as a tested combination, then verify volts, amps, watts and temperatures with a wattmeter — restrained aircraft, cowling on, body out of the prop arc.
LiPo discipline: correct charger mode and cell count, inspect for swelling, charge in a fire-safe place, storage-charge between sessions, follow the manufacturer for disposal.

Legal before the maiden

In the United States the 1.5 kg trainer is a registered aircraft, and the paperwork is quick, cheap, and worth doing before the first flight rather than after a conversation at the field.

US recreational flyers — the short list

  • Register if any of your models weighs 250 g or more: $5 for three years at FAADroneZone, one number covers all your recreational aircraft — mark it on the airframe, and carry proof of registration when you fly.
  • Pass TRUST (The Recreational UAS Safety Test): free, online, cannot be failed — but you must carry proof of completion when you fly.
  • Fly under the Recreational Exception, following the safety guidelines of a community-based organization such as the AMA.
  • Stay at or below 400 ft AGL in uncontrolled airspace; in controlled airspace get LAANC authorization first (most radio apps and sites link to it).
  • Remote ID: broadcast with a built-in or add-on module, or fly at a FRIA (FAA-Recognized Identification Area) — many AMA club fields are FRIAs, which is the simplest answer for a first model.

Rules change; the current version lives at faa.gov/uas/recreational_flyers. Outside the US, check your national aviation authority and local club — most countries have a similar registration-plus-basic-test pattern.

Preflight — the same order every time

Airframe → controls → power → radio. The printable version with check-off boxes is on the checklists page; use a written sequence because memory is weakest exactly when excitement is highest.

The maiden is an experiment, not a performance

  1. Conditions: big familiar field, mild steady wind, good light, experienced helper.
  2. Ground check: CG, directions, range test, power, timer, abort plan agreed aloud.
  3. Takeoff: smooth and shallow — no hero climbs.
  4. Height first: trim only at altitude, one axis at a time. If it needs lots of trim, land and inspect.
  5. Basic checks: gentle turns, power-off glide, control response. No low stall tests.
  6. Land early with a wide pattern and extra speed. Save the rest for flight two.
Abort if the plane is badly out of trim, controls feel wrong, power sounds weak, or the structure makes a new noise. Aborting is data; pressing on is gambling.

Trim and tune in a safe order

Mechanical zero → CG → throws and expo → aileron differential → power effects → flaps/mixes. One change at a time, and a flight log for every session: configuration, battery, wind, CG, trims, temperatures, observations, and the single next change.

Never keep flying an aircraft with large unexplained trim, any hint of flutter, overheating, or intermittent controls. Land. Find out. That is the whole discipline.
