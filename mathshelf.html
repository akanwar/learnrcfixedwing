The Math Shelf

Nothing here is memorized magic — every formula is a few lines of algebra away from L = W. This is the stretch material: read it when a chapter makes you curious.

The working set

q = ½ ρ V²   ·   L = q S CL   ·   D = q S CD   ·   Re = ρ V c / μ ≈ 68 000 · V · c   ·   AR = b²/S

q is the air’s “push strength” — note V is squared, which is why small speed changes matter so much. CL and CD package shape and angle of attack into unitless scores. (Definitions per NASA Glenn’s Beginner’s Guide.)

Stall speed, derived — not memorized

1  L = W (level flight)
2  W = ½ ρ V² S CL (substitute the lift equation)
3  slowest flight ⇒ CL = CLmax
4  Vs = √( 2W / ρ S CLmax )

Load factor in a bank, derived

1  bank φ tilts the lift with the wings
2  only L cos φ fights gravity ⇒ L cos φ = W
3  n ≡ L/W = 1 / cos φ
4  Vs ∝ √L  ⇒  Vs,turn = Vs √n
When you can re-derive a formula you own it: you know which assumptions it hides (steady flight, constant density, rigid wing) and exactly when it stops being true.
Your turnDerive the general level-flight speed for any CL, then compute the trainer’s speed at CL = 0.4 (W = 14.7 N, S = 0.333 m²).
V = √( 2W / ρ S CL ) — same derivation, just without setting CL to its max. Numbers: √(29.4 / (1.225 × 0.333 × 0.4)) ≈ 13.4 m/s.

The lift curve — the one graph to internalise

Induced drag: the price of lift

CD,i = CL² / ( π · AR · e )

Air spills around the tips, the wing flies in its own downwash, and the lift vector tilts back — that tilt is induced drag. CL is squared: fly twice the lift coefficient, pay four times the drag. AR divides: long wings pay less. e ≈ 0.7–0.85 for straight R/C wings.

The trainer at cruise (CL 0.5, AR 6.5, e 0.8): CD,i ≈ 0.015. In a slow pass at CL 1.1: 0.074 — five times more. Slow flight is draggy flight: throttle up before you slow down.

Your turnHalve the aspect ratio to 3.25 (same area) at CL 0.6. What happens to CD,i — and why does an albatross have AR ≈ 15?
CD,i doubles (AR is in the denominator): 0.36/(π·3.25·0.8) ≈ 0.044. The albatross lives at high CL for hours without flapping — induced drag is its whole energy budget, so evolution bought the longest spar it could grow.

Reynolds number: why small models feel different air

Re = ρ V c / μ ≈ 68 000 × V(m/s) × c(m)
RegimeReNotes
Indoor / micro2–7 ×10⁴Deep in the weird zone
Foam park flyer0.5–1.5 ×10⁵Low-Re sections earn their keep
The 1.5 kg trainer≈1.8 ×10⁵Comfortable, but landings dip lower
Giant scale0.5–1.5 ×10⁶Full-size data starts to apply
Full-size light aircraft3–8 ×10⁶A different world

Below ≈10⁵ the boundary layer stays laminar, separates early, and may not reattach: CLmax drops, drag rises, stalls sharpen, and full-size airfoil data quietly stops being true. Use sections designed for low Re, keep leading edges accurate, and trust UIUC’s low-speed tests over glossy polars.

Your turnCompute Re for the trainer’s wing on final approach: V = 1.3 Vs ≈ 10.5 m/s, c = 0.226 m. Inside the trouble zone?
Re ≈ 68 000 × 10.5 × 0.226 ≈ 161 000 — above the ≈10⁵ trouble zone, but with far less margin than cruise (≈184k). The flare is the squeeze: as the plane slows toward Vs (8.1 m/s), Re drops to ≈124 000 just as CL peaks — the lowest Reynolds number and the highest lift demand arrive at the same moment, a metre off the grass. That is why landings expose bad wings.
