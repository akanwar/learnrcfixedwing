Advanced Structures & Composite Techniques

Chapter 6 taught you to build straight and stiff. This volume puts numbers on strong: how big the loads really are, how to size a spar to carry them, and where a little fibreglass buys a lot of airplane.

How hard does the air actually pull?

In level flight the wing carries exactly the weight. In a firm pull-up or a gust it carries a multiple of it — the load factor n. Sport models are conventionally designed to about 6 g limit (you may fly it there) with a ×1.5 factor to 9 g ultimate (it breaks somewhere beyond). For the 1.5 kg trainer, a 6 g pull means the wing is lifting 9 kg.

Lift is spread across the span (roughly elliptically), so each wing panel behaves like a cantilever with its load centred about 42% of the way out. That sets the root bending moment — the number the spar must survive:

Mroot = n · W/2 · 0.424 · b/2 = 6 × 14.7/2 × 0.424 × 0.735 ≈ 13.8 N·m   (ultimate: ×1.5 ≈ 20.7 N·m)

The spar as an I-beam

Bending in a two-cap spar is just a force couple: cap force = moment ÷ cap separation. The trainer’s wing is 12% thick on a 226 mm chord → 27 mm deep; with caps inside the skins their centres sit ≈22 mm apart:

Fcap = 13.8 / 0.022 ≈ 630 N — sixty-four kilograms of pull on pieces of wood you can flex with two fingers

Now check a real cap. Spruce breaks at roughly 70 MPa along the grain; a 6×3 mm cap has 18 mm²:

σ = 630 N / 18 mm² ≈ 35 MPa → safety factor ≈ 2 at 6 g, ≈1.3 at the 9 g ultimate — a sensible model spar
Design moves, in order of value: deepen the spar (h is the cheapest strength there is — it’s why chapter 5 called thickness “room for a strong spar”), then widen the caps toward the root where M peaks, then taper everything toward the tip where M → 0. The shear web is not optional: without it the caps are just two sticks that fold independently. Balsa web, grain vertical.

The carbon alternative — a 10 mm × 8 mm pultruded tube as a one-piece spar:

I = π(D⁴−d⁴)/64 = 2.9×10−10 m⁴  →  σ = M·c/I = 13.8 × 0.005 / 2.9×10−10 ≈ 238 MPa vs ≈600+ MPa for pultruded carbon → SF ≈ 2.5

The same tube doubles as the wing joiner on a two-piece wing — and Deep Dive 4 will bolt a printed wing around exactly this spar. Carbon fails suddenly and splinters; wear eye protection cutting it, and never trust a tube that has already taken a hit.

Your turnScale the trainer to 2 kg at the same span. What happens to the cap stress — and what is the cheapest fix?
M scales with weight: 13.8 × 2/1.5 ≈ 18.3 N·m → cap force ≈ 835 N → σ ≈ 46 MPa: your 6 g safety factor drops to 1.5 and ultimate is no longer covered. Cheapest fix is geometric, not material: a 14% thick section (or a deeper spar bay) moves the caps to ≈26 mm and brings stress back to ≈39 MPa — before you add a single gram of wood.

Torsion and the D-box

Bending is only half the job. Aileron loads and the airfoil’s pitching moment (chapter 5 — remember the S1223’s big Cm) twist the wing, and a twisted wing flutters or reverses its ailerons at speed. Skins fight twist, and closed cells fight it best: sheet the leading edge back to the spar, top and bottom, and the skin + web form a closed “D” tube — stiffness jumps an order of magnitude over an open structure. That is why so many classic wings are D-boxed even when the aft bays are open ribs and covering.

Composites where they count

You do not need a mould shop. On a wood or foam model, cloth + epoxy is a reinforcement, applied exactly where loads concentrate:

ClothWeightUse
Light glass25–49 g/m²sealing wood, wear surfaces, hinge-line beef-up
Medium glass80–110 g/m²fuselage nose, firewall box, wing centre-section joint
Heavy glass / carbon160–200 g/m² (carbon 200)landing-gear blocks, one-piece wing joins, high-stress hardpoints

Working rules that save projects: mix epoxy by weight to the maker’s ratio (kitchen scales, ±1 g); a wet layup roughly doubles the cloth weight in finished mass (80 g/m² cloth → ≈160 g/m² on the airframe), so a 2 dm² nose patch costs ≈3 g — cheap for what it buys; orient fibres along the load — 0° spanwise for bending caps, ±45° for torsion skins; and squeegee out every gram of resin the cloth doesn’t need — resin-rich is heavy, not strong. Foam-core wings with glass skins are the same I-beam logic as the spar: skins are the caps, foam is a full-chord shear web.

Epoxy and cut carbon are sensitising: nitrile gloves, ventilation, and a real dust mask when sanding cured laminate. Uncured epoxy allergies are earned once and kept for life.
Your turnYour trainer’s wing joins in the middle with a 100 mm-wide glass wrap. Which cloth, how many grams, and which fibre direction?
The joint carries the full root bending — this is a hardpoint, so medium-to-heavy cloth: one wrap of 160 g/m² around the 226 mm chord × 100 mm width ≈ 0.045 m² → ≈7 g cloth, ≈14 g finished. Fibres mainly 0° (spanwise) to bridge the join like spar caps, ideally with a ±45° layer under it for torsion. Fourteen grams to make one wing from two — the best mass you’ll ever spend.
