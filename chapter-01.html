Chapter 01

Design the System

The pilot commands rotations; physics decides what happens next. This chapter builds the vocabulary the whole guide runs on.

Write the mission before drawing the airplane

Every good design answers a question. Write yours down with numbers, not adjectives: target all-up mass, a stall speed you can land at, field size, wind you will accept, budget, build time. Pick one primary mission and at most two secondary goals. Each extra requirement has a mass and drag bill attached.

Your turnWrite your own mission in three lines: primary job, all-up mass target, and the field you fly from. What did you have to give up?
There is no single right answer — the exercise is noticing the tradeoffs. A small park demands low wing loading far more than top speed; a windy hillside demands the opposite.

Four forces — and the moments they create

Lift acts roughly perpendicular to the relative wind; weight acts through the centre of gravity; thrust and drag act roughly along the flight path. But forces act at locations: a force away from the CG also creates a turning moment. Steady flight needs both force balance and moment balance — lift can equal weight while the airplane still pitches uncontrollably.

A tail is not merely a small rear wing. It supplies leverage that balances and damps pitching and yawing motion. Chapter 3 turns that leverage into numbers.

Pitch, roll, yaw — and who commands them

AxisMain controlPilot seesStabilised by
PitchElevatorNose up or downHorizontal tail
RollAileronsBank left or rightDihedral, wing damping
YawRudderNose left or rightVertical fin

A normal turn is a three-axis event: ailerons bank, elevator holds the curved path, rudder keeps the nose from skidding. And banking has a price:

n = 1 / cos φ     Vs,turn = Vs √n

At 60° of bank the wing carries twice the weight and stall speed rises 41%. Hence the oldest rule at the field: no steep, slow turns near the ground.

Your turnAt what bank angle does stall speed rise by 25%?
Multiplier 1.25 means n = 1.25² = 1.5625, so cos φ = 1/1.5625 = 0.64 → φ ≈ 50°. Derivation on the math shelf.

Conventional-trainer starting points, not laws. Authority comes from area, lever arm, deflection, airspeed, stiffness, and clean airflow — not area alone.

Linkages: geometry beats muscle

A rigid, slop-free linkage makes a modest servo work better than a powerful servo connected badly. Inner hole on the servo arm = more force, less travel; the control-horn hole stays close to the hinge line; servo arm at 90° to the pushrod at neutral; no flexing rods, loose clevises, or soft mounts.

Flutter risk rises quickly with speed. Stiff surfaces, tight hinges, short rigid linkages, and adequate servo torque are structural safety items — not cosmetic details.
