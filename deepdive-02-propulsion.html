Electric Propulsion & Propeller Matching

Chapter 4 decided the trainer needs 225 W. This volume is about making those watts real: choosing the motor, prop and battery as a matched system — with numbers you can check before anything spins.

Follow the watts

Battery → ESC → motor → prop → air. Every stage taxes you: a healthy hobby outrunner converts roughly 75–80% of electrical watts to shaft watts; the prop converts shaft watts to thrust at an efficiency that depends entirely on how well you matched it. The trainer’s chain, computed before buying anything:

225 W ÷ 11.1 V = 20.3 A  ·  ESC ≥ 20.3 × 1.35 → 30 A  ·  2200 mAh at 20.3 A = 9.2C — any honest 25C pack loafs

Kv, decoded

Kv is not a power rating (chapter 7’s warning) — it is unloaded rpm per volt. Load it with a prop and rpm sags to roughly 75–85% of Kv × V. That single fact sizes the whole system:

1000 Kv × 11.1 V × 0.80 ≈ 8 900 rpm under load

Now the prop’s pitch turns rpm into a speed. A 10×6 “screws forward” 6 inches per turn (chapter 7):

pitch speed = 8 900/60 × 0.152 m ≈ 22.6 m/s

Check it against the airframe — this is the matching step most people skip:

Rule of thumbTrainer numbersVerdict
Pitch speed ≈ 2.5–3 × stall speed2.5–3 × 8.1 = 20–24 m/s vs 22.6✓ right in the slot
Cruise ≈ half of pitch speed12 / 22.6 = 53%✓ relaxed cruise throttle
Thrust ≈ 0.6–0.8 × weight for a trainercomputed below: 0.72✓

The three rules agreeing is not luck — it is what “matched” means. A 2200 Kv motor on the same 3S would demand a tiny 6×4 prop screaming at 20k rpm: same watts, worse efficiency, no climb authority. High Kv + small prop suits fast models; low Kv + big prop suits everything else.

Static thrust from first principles

A propeller is a momentum machine: it throws a column of air backwards. Ideal momentum theory gives the best any disc can do; real props deliver about 70% of it (their “figure of merit”). For the trainer’s 10-inch prop:

disc area A = π(0.127)² = 0.0507 m²  ·  shaft power ≈ 225 × 0.75 = 169 W
Tideal = (2 ρ A P²)1/3 = (2 × 1.225 × 0.0507 × 169²)1/3 ≈ 15.2 N
Tstatic ≈ 0.7 × 15.2 ≈ 10.7 N ≈ 1.09 kgf  →  thrust/weight = 10.7 / 14.7 = 0.72

Which is exactly the 0.6–0.8 a trainer wants: enough to climb out of trouble, not enough to tempt vertical nonsense on a maiden.

Why diameter beats pitch: absorbed power scales roughly with diameter to the fourth power at fixed rpm — one inch more prop (10→11) is a 1.46× power jump. And a bigger, slower disc accelerates more air more gently, which is aerodynamically cheaper: for climb and cruise efficiency, run the largest prop that clears the ground and load it with a low-Kv motor. Small fast props exist for speed, not efficiency.
Your turnSame 225 W through a 12-inch prop instead of the 10. What does momentum theory promise for static thrust — and what stops you from just fitting a 14?
A grows by (12/10)² = 1.44×; T scales with A1/3 at fixed power, so ×1.441/3 ≈ 1.13 → ≈ 12.1 N and T/W ≈ 0.82. The stops: ground clearance, the motor’s rpm sagging below the pitch-speed rule (the 12×6 loads the motor far harder — check amps!), and the d⁴ law turning “just one more inch” into an ESC fire. Change one variable, then re-measure.

The chart beats the math because props vary: two “10×6” props from different makers can load a motor 15% apart. The math tells you which chart row to start at and whether a measurement is plausible — the wattmeter has the final word. Chapter 7’s bench-test safety rules apply to every run.

Batteries without the marketing

The trainer draws 9.2C from its 2200 mAh pack — any genuine 25C cell delivers that without noticeable sag. C-ratings on the label are marketing (chapter 7: “a claim, not a promise”); what you can verify is voltage under load on the wattmeter: a healthy 3S should hold above ≈10.5 V at full throttle. Sagging under 9.9 V (3.3 V/cell) under this mild load means a tired or dishonest pack. Endurance math stays as chapter 4 computed it: 8.8 min mixed throttle, 5.2 min wide open.

Your turnYour wattmeter reads 26 A and 275 W on the “same” 10×6 from another maker. What do you do — and what was the clue that this could happen?
That prop loads 22% over plan — beyond the ESC’s comfortable margin (26 × 1.35 ≈ 35 A > 30) and the motor’s 3 W/g class. Drop to a 9×6 or the original brand, and re-measure. The clue: step 4’s warning — prop brands differ by double-digit percentages, which is exactly why measurement is a step and not a suggestion.
