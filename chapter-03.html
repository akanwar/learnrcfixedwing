Chapter 03

Tail & Balance

CG position can turn a well-built airplane from calm to uncontrollable. This chapter is the difference between a maiden and a mourning.

Tail volume: area × leverage

A small tail far behind the CG can do the job of a big tail mounted close. That product of area and lever arm, normalised by the wing, is the tail volume coefficient — the screening tool used from trainers to airliners.

Vh = Sh lh / (S c̄)   →   Sh = Vh S c̄ / lh
Vv = Sv lv / (S b)   →   Sv = Vv S b / lv
Broad rangeForgiving trainer start
Horizontal Vh0.30–0.600.50–0.60
Vertical Vv0.02–0.050.035–0.05

A longer fuselage buys a smaller tail. Lots of side area ahead of the CG (big canopy, floats) may need more fin behind it. Volume coefficients are screening tools, not proof of stability — compare with successful aircraft of similar layout. (Source: MIT Unified Engineering.)

Your turnYour fuselage came out 10 cm shorter than planned (arm 550 mm instead of 650). Recalculate the trainer’s Sh (Vh 0.55, S 0.333 m², c̄ 0.226 m).
Sh = 0.55 × 0.333 × 0.226 / 0.55 = 0.0753 m² ≈ 753 cm² — about 18% more tail because you lost 15% of the arm. Leverage is cheap; area costs weight at the worst address (the tail).

CG, neutral point, static margin

Every aircraft has a neutral point (NP): the CG position where it would be exactly indifferent in pitch. Keep the CG ahead of the NP and the plane resists disturbances; let it drift behind and no amount of thumbs will save it.

SM = ( xNP − xCG ) / c̄

CG too far forward

  • Very stable but nose-heavy; weak flare
  • More trim drag, faster landings

CG too far aft

  • Pitch-sensitive, hard to trim, may oscillate
  • Behind the NP: unrecoverably unstable
Balance the complete, flight-ready aircraft — battery, prop, hatch, landing gear installed. Move the battery or equipment before adding lead; a heavy tail needs a much bigger nose correction.
Your turnc̄ = 226 mm and you balance the plane 12 mm ahead of the NP. Static margin? Safe for a maiden?
SM = 12/226 ≈ 5% — flyable but nervous; too little for a first flight. Move the CG forward to 10–15% (23–34 mm ahead of the NP) and shift it aft only after calm, trimmed flights.

Stability ≠ authority

Stability is the tendency to return to trim after a disturbance. Authority is the muscle to command a change. You need both, in the right amounts: a stable plane can still run out of elevator in the flare, and an authoritative one can be trimmed so badly it fights you every second. Verify both: natural stability and enough control for takeoff, flare, recovery, and crosswinds.
